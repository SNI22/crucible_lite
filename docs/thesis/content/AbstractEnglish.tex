\chapter*{\rm\bfseries Abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Abstract}

Robotic grasping of thin, deformable fabric remains an unsolved problem
in industrial automation. Two competing gripper geometries are routinely
proposed: rigid parallel-jaw fingers, which offer precise force control
but narrow operational tolerance, and compliant finray-effect fingers,
which trade force precision for a wider operational envelope. This
thesis presents an experimental evaluation of that trade on the task of
picking a single flat cloth off a surface, comparing six gripper
variants (two rigid parallel-jaw configurations and four 3D-printed
finray configurations) on a Franka Research 3 arm. Press depth and tilt
angle were chosen as the evaluation axes because they are the placement
errors a deployed system cannot fully control: a low-accuracy or
coarsely calibrated arm carries millimetre-scale surface-height error
and degree-scale orientation error, and the gripper must absorb both.
Stationary contact force was the primary outcome and grasp success the
secondary outcome.

Across 845 analysed trials, the rigid jaws held cloth only within a
$1.5$\,mm depth window ($2.5$\,mm with a compliant pad), bounded by a
force safety limit, whereas every finray variant grasped across the
full $10$\,mm calibrated analysis window at every orientation tested.
Two depth-gated effects emerged. Finray contact force is strongly
tilt-sensitive at the contact boundary but robust to tilt once pressed
past the buckling knee, and the direction-dependent handedness about
the finger's geometrically asymmetric axis (a factor of $2.36$ at
boundary depth) vanishes at safe operating depths. Compliant grippers
therefore achieve directional robustness only when pressed past a
threshold depth; control policies that operate near the contact
boundary for delicate fabric handling must model the gripper's
directional asymmetry explicitly.
